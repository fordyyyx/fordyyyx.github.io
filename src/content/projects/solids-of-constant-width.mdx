## What is constant width?

A solid of constant width is exactly what it sounds like: however you turn it,
its width stays the same. The obvious examples are the circle in two dimensions
and the sphere in three.

The surprise is that they aren't the only ones. The simplest other example is
the Reuleaux triangle: a triangle with curved sides. Hold it between two
parallel lines and it can turn freely, touching both lines the whole way round.

## Building the shapes

To draw a Reuleaux triangle, take an equilateral triangle and draw a circle
around each corner, with a radius equal to the triangle's side. The curved
shape where all three circles overlap is the Reuleaux triangle.

As part of the project I built this in Wolfram Mathematica, which only takes
one line: intersect three unit disks, centred on three evenly spaced points on
a circle of radius <Maths tex="\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}" />.

```text
RegionIntersection @@ (Disk /@ CirclePoints[1/Sqrt[3], 3]) //
  RegionBoundary //
  Region
```

The same ideas work in three dimensions, and the project included 3D-printed
models. Spin a Reuleaux triangle around a line of symmetry and you get a solid
of constant width. A tetrahedron is trickier. The natural guess is the Reuleaux
tetrahedron, where each face is a Reuleaux triangle, but it isn't quite constant
width. The Meissner tetrahedron fixes this by replacing three of the edges with
a surface made by rotating an arc of a circle.

## Why it matters: coins

Most coins are circles, like the 2p and the £2. The 50p and 20p aren't: they're
Reuleaux heptagons, built like a Reuleaux triangle but with seven arcs instead
of three.

That shape is a practical choice. Coin-counting machines and self-checkouts
often need coins to roll, and they can tell coins apart by measuring their
width. A constant width means a coin measures the same whichever way it lands.

The 12-sided £1 coin looks as though it might work the same way, but it can't.
Each corner of a Reuleaux polygon needs an edge opposite it, and an even number
of sides puts a corner opposite another corner instead. Its width
[varies between 23.03mm and 23.43mm](https://plus.maths.org/content/new-1-coin-gets-even),
so before its release there was concern about whether machines would cope.

Try it yourself: change the number of sides, then turn the shape. It always
touches both lines. Keep an eye on the perimeter too.

## Barbier's theorem

Here's the result at the centre of my poster: **every shape of constant width
has a perimeter of π multiplied by its width**, whatever its exact shape.

So a Reuleaux triangle of width 1 and a circle of radius 0.5 look nothing alike,
but both have a perimeter of exactly π. The proof is my favourite part, because
it comes from probability rather than geometry: it involves dropping needles.

<details>
<summary>See the proof</summary>

**Buffon's needle.** Imagine a floor with infinitely many parallel lines, each a
distance <Maths tex="D" /> apart. Drop a needle of length <Maths tex="l" />,
shorter than <Maths tex="D" />. What's the probability that it crosses a line?

Let <Maths tex="y \in [0, D)" /> be the height of the needle's lower end above
a line, and <Maths tex="\theta \in [0, \pi)" /> the angle it makes with the
horizontal. The needle crosses the line above it when
<Maths tex="y \le l \sin\theta" />. If both values are uniformly distributed,
the probability is the area under that curve divided by the area of the
<Maths tex="\pi \times D" /> rectangle:

<Maths tex="A = \int_0^\pi l \sin\theta \, d\theta = 2l \implies P = \frac{2l}{\pi D}" block />

**Buffon's noodle.** Now let the needle bend, and let it be longer than
<Maths tex="D" />. To handle this, split the noodle into a chain of
<Maths tex="n" /> straight segments, each shorter than <Maths tex="D" />, with
lengths <Maths tex="l_1, l_2, \dots, l_n" />. Let <Maths tex="N_i" /> be the
number of times segment <Maths tex="i" /> crosses a line, so the total number of
crossings is <Maths tex="N = N_1 + N_2 + \dots + N_n" />.

Each short segment is a needle, so its expected number of crossings is
<Maths tex="\frac{2l_i}{\pi D}" />. Expectations add up even when the segments
are joined together, so:

<Maths tex="\begin{aligned} E(N) &= \frac{2l_1}{\pi D} + \frac{2l_2}{\pi D} + \dots + \frac{2l_n}{\pi D} \\ &= \frac{2(l_1 + l_2 + \dots + l_n)}{\pi D} = \frac{2l}{\pi D} \end{aligned}" block />

This doesn't depend on the angles between the segments, so we can let
<Maths tex="n \to \infty" /> and the formula holds for a smooth curve. The
expected number of crossings depends only on the noodle's length.

**The proof.** Take a noodle shaped like a circle of radius
<Maths tex="r" />, and space the lines <Maths tex="D = 2r" /> apart. However it
lands, it crosses exactly twice, which matches the formula:

<Maths tex="E(N) = \frac{2l}{\pi D} = \frac{2 \cdot 2\pi r}{\pi \cdot 2r} = 2" block />

A closed noodle of any shape with constant width <Maths tex="D" /> also crosses
exactly twice: either once on each of two neighbouring lines, or twice on one.
This time, solve the same formula for its length:

<Maths tex="\frac{2l}{\pi D} = 2 \implies l = \pi D" block />

That's the same perimeter as the circle, whatever the shape. This proves
Barbier's theorem.

</details>
